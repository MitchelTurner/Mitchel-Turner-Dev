Why one bench

Hardware projects stall at the handoff.

The schematic can be right and the layout can be right. The project still stalls where each layer belongs to a different person, and everyone's piece works while the system does not.

The usual chain
  • An electrical engineer sends you the gerbers and moves on
  • A firmware contractor writes drivers from the datasheet
  • An app developer gets the telemetry format by email
  • The path from sensor to screen has no owner
  • It works on the bench and fails in the field

Every seam is a place for the spec to get lost, and every fix crosses three invoices.

This one
  • One person picks the microcontroller and routes the board
  • That same person writes the driver for the part they chose
  • They define the telemetry format
  • They build the software the data lands in
  • A field failure can be traced across all four layers

One person is a limit on how much can be in flight at once. It is also why a failure in the field can be traced the same day.

How a project runs

Priced at the schematic, not at fab.

Hardware punishes surprises. Each step ends with something you can hold, read, or run before the next one starts.

  1. Step 01 · 1–2 wks

    Requirements

    What it must do, where it has to survive, what it can weigh, and what a unit can cost.

  2. Step 02 · 2–3 wks

    Schematic and BOM

    Parts chosen for availability and compliance, not only the datasheet. Priced at each quantity before layout starts.

  3. Step 03 · 3–5 wks

    Layout and fab

    Routing, a manufacturing review, then boards ordered and built. Lead times are named before you commit.

  4. Step 04 · 3–6 wks

    Bring-up and firmware

    Power, one rail at a time, then drivers, then the application. Written down as it happens.

  5. Step 05 · field

    Test in the weather

    Real range, real rain, real water. A result on the bench is a guess until then.

Straight answer

A board revision costs weeks. A code revision costs an afternoon.

Hardware takes longer and costs more than software because a board change means a minimum order and another round of bring-up. The first question is whether the job can be done on hardware that already exists: an off-the-shelf flight controller, a commercial sensor, someone else's radio. Often it can, and I will say so.

A custom board earns its keep when the part you need does not exist, cannot be sourced under the rules you have to follow, costs too much at your quantity, or will not survive where you are putting it. I have hit all four. They are rarer than the first sketch suggests.
